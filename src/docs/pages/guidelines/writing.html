Writing

How Vici's pages, prototypes and guides are worded. The rules are for Victor and for the agents that build with him, and the build checks the ones it can.

Voice

Write like a colleague explaining work they did: plain words, short sentences and the specific thing. If a sentence would fit any product, rewrite it until it fits only this one.

Do
Shows the 128 invoices the agent couldn't match

A number and a noun from the prototype. Nobody could put this heading on another product.

Don't
Turn your invoices into insight

It would fit any product, so it tells the reader nothing about this one.

  • The docs site is in British English: colour, licence, organise, grey.
  • A prototype speaks its audience's language, British English or German, from the guide to the error messages. See German.
  • Address the reader as “you”. In these docs that is whoever builds with Vici; in a prototype, the person using it.
  • Use the active voice and say who does what: “The agent matched 342 invoices”, not “342 invoices were matched”.
  • Contractions are fine: isn't, you'll, doesn't.
  • Cut words that change nothing: simply, just, really, in order to, please note.
  • Mark what a model wrote with the AI feature tone, so readers know which content is generated.

Headings and labels

Readers scan before they read, so headings and labels carry the page. Each one names its subject in a few plain words.

  • Write headings, buttons, tabs, menu items and table headers in sentence case. Where a style shows capitals, such as table column headers, CSS sets them; see Typography.
  • A page's title, its navigation label and the links to it use the same words.
  • A heading names what follows. No questions (“Why Vici?”), puns or taglines.
  • A lead is one or two sentences, at most 40 words, and says what the page or section helps you do.
  • Buttons start with a verb and say what happens: “Export CSV”, “Save changes”. Places are nouns: “Settings”, “Reports”. “How it works” is the one fixed exception, because it opens the guide on every prototype.
  • Links name where they go: “the accessibility rules”, not “learn more” or “click here”.
  • Errors say what happened and what to do next: “The file is larger than 20 MB. Choose a smaller file or compress it.”
  • Empty states say what is missing and what comes next: “No invoices need a person today. New ones arrive after the morning run.”

Names, not record ids

A prototype's data gives every record an id: vendor 10003, agreement RA-CISCO, company code CC-1000. The visitor knows those things by name, so the screen says Meraki, the Cisco agreement and Frankfurt. The ids stay in the data, the engine, the tools and the logs.

Do
Splunk bills on its own account, so the year-end report never counts it

A name the room knows, and what follows from it.

Don't
Vendor 10005 has no KONZS for RA-CISCO

Three ids and a field name. Only the person who built the data can read it.

  • One module turns ids into names, places and words, and everything that writes the screen goes through it, the agent's words and its drafts included. A new screen then can't forget to.
  • Several names in one breath take their short form: “Meraki, Acacia and Splunk”, not three legal names. Where two short forms would clash, keep the word that tells them apart.
  • A draft for someone who works in another system names what to change in words: “set the Cisco group key”, not “KONZS = CISCO”.
  • A prototype's tests run recordIdProblems(text, ids) from scripts/lib/ids.mjs over the text of every screen, with the ids of its own data. Each id that still reaches the screen comes back as a problem.

Short forms and unfamiliar names

A prototype's subject brings its own short forms and names: BStatG and MiLoAufzV for two laws, PflichtenLichten for a federal plan, Z1 for a reason code. Whoever works with them reads them at a glance. The room reads them once, from a distance, and stops there.

Do
The trade and production reports ask for the same 4 items

The reports in words. The short titles are a hover away.

Don't
§ 11 HdlDlStatG and § 9 ProdGewStatG overlap, reason Z1

Two short titles and a code. Only someone who works with these laws can read it.

  • A short form the room can't expand doesn't carry a screen. Write the name in words, or show the figure first and put the short form on hover and in the text a screen reader reads, as Duty Radar's law map does with the titles of 31 laws.
  • Where the short form is the evidence, as in a citation, it stays beside the name in words and links to the source: “Trade and services statistics, § 11”, with § 11 HdlDlStatG on hover.
  • A name the room has never heard says what it is in plain words where it first appears: “PflichtenLichten is the federal and state governments' plan to take duties off businesses.”
  • A code from the data's own vocabulary, such as a reason code, reads as its words: “because both ask for the same data”, not “Z1”. The code stays in the data and the logs, as record ids do.

  • Use figures for anything counted or measured: 3 suppliers, 128 invoices, 5 min. In running prose, one to nine can be words when nothing is measured, as in “two options”.
  • Put a no-break space (&nbsp;) between a number and its unit, the percent sign included, so the two never wrap apart. This follows SI usage, and German style asks for it too.
  • Round to what the reader needs, and keep the same number of decimals within a column: 72.8 %, not 72.8125 %.
  • A figure that rests on part of the data, or on a side the sources leave open, says so in words beside it: “77 on 5 of 6 layers”, “level 2, one side not in the sources”. A question mark after a number (“2?”) leaves the reader to guess what is missing.
  • Set columns of numbers, counters and anything that updates in tabular figures. Vici's table cells with .vc-num, stats and badges already use them; elsewhere add .vc-tabular.
  • Use the minus sign (−) for negative numbers and the multiplication sign for dimensions: 640 × 640 px.
  • Keep ISO dates (2026-11-19) for exports, file names and code.

Punctuation

  • Use curly quotation marks in prose: “double”, with ‘single’ inside. Code keeps straight quotes. German uses „these“; see Typesetting German.
  • Don't use dashes as punctuation in English sentences. A comma, a colon, brackets or a full stop does the job. The en dash joins ranges: 10–20 px, P0–P3.
  • Leave exclamation marks out of interface text.
  • Leave out the serial comma (red, green and blue) unless the sentence would be misread without it.

German

Some prototypes and demos are in German. A German surface is German throughout: the guide, buttons, table headers, errors and the byline. Product names and established terms, such as Dashboard or Agentic AI, stay as they are.

  • Set lang="de" on <html>, or on the element when a German part sits inside an English page.
  • Prefer labels that address no one: “Bericht exportieren”. Where a sentence has to address the reader, use “Sie”.
  • Write buttons as infinitives or short commands without a pronoun: “Exportieren”, “Weiter”.
  • Join compounds, and hyphenate around English parts: “Lieferantenrabatt”, “Agentic-AI-Showcase”.
  • Numbers and dates follow the German column of the number formats. Quotation marks, dashes and hyphenation are covered in Typesetting German.
